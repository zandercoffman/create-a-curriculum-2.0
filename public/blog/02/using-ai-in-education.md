# Using AI in education: support the thinking, don't replace it

Create-A-Curriculum uses AI to draft curriculums, so I'd be a hypocrite to stay quiet about the risks. A tool that writes a lesson plan in seconds can write a learner's essay in seconds too, and the second job is where learning can quietly stop.

The research on this is mixed. Some studies show AI hurting learning, and others show it helping a lot. What separates them is what the AI does and what the learner is left doing. This post covers why effort matters, what the studies found on both sides, how to set up AI use at home or in a classroom, and where I'm still unsure.

## Why the effort matters

Learning mostly happens in the hard part of a task: trying to recall something, getting it wrong, noticing why, and trying again. Reading a finished solution feels like understanding it. Producing one is how you find out whether you do.

Cognitive scientists have tested a version of this for a long time. In a well-known 2006 experiment, Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke had students study short prose passages and then either reread them or take recall tests. On a test five minutes later, the students who had reread did better. On tests two days and a week later, the students who had been tested remembered substantially more, even though rereading had left them feeling more confident that they knew the material. A few years later, a survey of 177 undergraduates found that more than 80% listed rereading notes as one of their main ways to study. It's a comfortable habit, and it builds less than testing yourself does.

That gap between how learning feels and how much sticks matters a lot for AI. Researchers separate performance, which is how well you do on a task right now, from learning, which is what you can still do later without help. A learner who has an AI solve every problem will turn in excellent work on every problem. Whether anything stuck only shows up when the AI is gone.

That's the trouble with an answer machine at a kitchen table or in a classroom. It removes the struggle that does the teaching, and it does it so smoothly that the learner and the adult both feel fine about it.

## What the research shows

Most of this work is new, short term, and limited to particular subjects and age groups. I'd read it as evidence about design choices more than a verdict on AI.

### When the AI did the task for the learner

A field experiment from Wharton and the University of Pennsylvania, published in PNAS in 2025, is the clearest example. Nearly 1,000 high school math students in Turkey practiced with one of two AI tools, or with none. One tool worked like a standard chatbot, and the other had safeguards built in so it guided students instead of handing over solutions. During practice, the chatbot group scored 48% better than the group with no AI, and the guided group scored 127% better. Then the AI was taken away for an exam. The guided group scored about the same as the no-AI group, and the chatbot group scored 17% worse. The authors read this as students leaning on the chatbot instead of learning the concepts, and as evidence that safeguards reduce the harm.

An MIT Media Lab preprint looked at writing. Fifty-four university students wrote essays using ChatGPT, a search engine, or nothing, while their brain activity was recorded with EEG. The ChatGPT group showed weaker connectivity during the task. When asked to quote from their own essay, 83% of that group couldn't, compared with about 11% in the other two groups. Only 18 people completed the fourth session, the paper hasn't been peer reviewed, and other scientists have cautioned against reading too much into it. The lead author did say that timing may matter a lot, meaning when in the process a learner brings the AI in.

A Microsoft Research and Carnegie Mellon survey asked 319 knowledge workers who use generative AI at least weekly how they apply critical thinking to its output. Higher confidence in the AI went with less critical thinking, and higher confidence in their own skills went with more. These were adults at work and the data was self-reported, so it doesn't measure learning. It still matches a pattern a teacher would recognize, which is that the people least sure of a task are the ones most likely to accept whatever the AI says about it. Learners are, by definition, the least sure.

### When the AI was built to make the learner work

A randomized trial at Harvard compared an AI tutor with an in-class active learning session in an introductory physics course. Each of the 194 students learned one topic from the tutor and another in class. They learned significantly more with the tutor, in less time, and said they felt more engaged and motivated. The tutor wasn't a plain chatbot. It was built around the course's own teaching method, which has students struggle with a problem before any direct explanation comes. It was also tested at one university and on two topics, so I wouldn't assume it carries over to an eight-year-old learning fractions.

A World Bank trial in Nigeria put students in nine public secondary schools into a six-week after-school program. They worked in pairs with GPT-4 under teacher supervision, using prompts designed to promote reasoning. The students gained about 0.3 standard deviations in English, which the authors estimate is equal to a year and a half to two years of typical schooling, and they also did better on end-of-year exams. Each extra session a student attended helped more. The authors credit the structure: a set program, clear pedagogical guardrails, and active teacher facilitation.

A Stanford trial tried a third approach. Tutor CoPilot didn't talk to students at all. It suggested strategies to human tutors during live math sessions. Across more than 700 tutors and 1,000 students, students were 4 percentage points more likely to master a topic, and students of lower-rated tutors gained 9 points. The tool cost about $20 per tutor per year. Tutors also reported that some suggestions weren't appropriate for the student's grade level, so the human judgment was still doing real work.

### What the studies have in common

| Study | Learners | What the AI did | What happened |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Wharton and Penn (PNAS, 2025) | About 1,000 high school math students | Chatbot gave solutions, or a tutor version guided | Chatbot users scored 17% worse on the exam without AI. Guided users matched the control group. |
| MIT Media Lab (preprint, 2025) | 54 university students | Helped write essays | Lower brain connectivity and poorer recall of their own essay. Small and not peer reviewed. |
| Microsoft and Carnegie Mellon (2025) | 319 knowledge workers | Assisted with everyday work tasks | More confidence in AI went with less critical thinking. Self-reported. |
| Harvard physics (Scientific Reports, 2025) | 194 undergraduates | Tutor built to make students try first | Learned more in less time than in an active learning class. |
| World Bank, Nigeria (2025) | Secondary students in 9 schools | Supervised, structured GPT-4 sessions | About 0.3 standard deviations of gain in English over six weeks. |
| Stanford Tutor CoPilot (2025) | 700+ tutors, 1,000+ students | Coached human tutors | 4 points more mastery, 9 points for students of lower-rated tutors. |

In the studies that found harm, the learner could get finished answers with nothing around it. In the studies that found gains, the AI was set up to question and hint, or an adult was involved, or both. That pattern is why I keep coming back to the rule in the next section.

## A rule that holds up

The learner thinks first, and AI comes in after. It can ask questions, give hints, check work, and explain a second way. It shouldn't be the one producing the thing the learner is supposed to be practicing.

| Task | AI that supports the thinking | AI that replaces it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Math practice | Gives a hint after you've shown your attempt | Solves the problem and shows the steps |
| Writing | Asks questions about your draft and points out unclear spots | Writes the draft or rewrites yours |
| Reading | Explains a hard passage after you've tried it | Summarizes the chapter so you skip it |
| Studying | Quizzes you and waits for your answer | Hands you a study guide to reread |
| Research | Suggests where to look and what to ask | Gives conclusions with no sources to check |
| Language learning | Corrects your sentence and explains the rule | Translates for you so you never attempt it |

The same rule applies to adults. When I use AI to draft a curriculum, the planning decisions are still mine.

## Habits that keep the learner thinking

1. Try first. Make a real attempt before asking for anything, and write down what you tried. If the attempt is blank, the AI is doing the work from the start. This is also the one the MIT author's comment about timing points toward.
2. Ask for hints, not answers. Say so in the prompt, because most chatbots default to giving the answer. The guided tutors in the studies above worked because they were built to withhold it.
3. Get quizzed. Having the AI ask you questions one at a time and wait for your response is closer to studying than asking it to explain the topic. It's the testing effect from the start of this post, with a patient quizmaster.
4. Explain it back. Close the screen and say or write the idea in your own words. If you can't, you didn't learn it yet.
5. Check the AI. It can be wrong and sound sure. Compare facts against a textbook, a teacher, or a primary source. The first version of this site carried a note that the AI can make mistakes, and the reason hasn't changed.
6. Come back later. Redo a similar problem the next day, or quiz yourself two days out. The 2006 retention results suggest that's when you find out what stuck.
7. Keep some work AI-free. Handwritten first drafts, talking through a problem out loud, and quizzes without a screen all show what the learner actually knows.

Here are prompts to copy. Change the brackets.

```
I'm learning [topic]. Don't give me answers. Ask me one question at a time to
find out what I already know, and give me a hint only if I get stuck.
```

```
Here is my attempt at this problem and my reasoning: [paste it]. Point out
where my thinking goes wrong, but don't rewrite it or solve it for me.
```

```
Quiz me on [topic] with five questions, one at a time. Wait for my answer
before telling me whether it's right, and explain what I missed.
```

```
Here is my draft: [paste it]. Don't edit it. Tell me which sentence is least
clear and ask me one question that would help me fix it.
```

```
I'll explain [topic] in my own words. When I'm done, tell me what I got right,
what I got wrong, and what I left out. Don't explain it until I finish.
```

```
Write a short paragraph about [topic] that contains exactly one factual
mistake. Don't tell me where it is. I'll try to find it.
```

The last one is a fun exercise for checking skills. Finding a planted mistake is practice for finding the unplanted ones.

## A worked example

Say a ninth grader is stuck on a system of equations. Here's the flow with every habit in it.

She does the problem on paper first and gets as far as setting up substitution before she freezes. She writes down where. She pastes her work into the hint-only prompt, and the AI asks what happens if she solves the first equation for y. She tries it, finishes the problem herself, and checks it by plugging the answer back in. Then she asks for one similar problem and does it without the AI. The next morning, before looking at anything, she explains the method out loud to a parent. Two days later, she gets a short quiz with the AI waiting for each answer.

That sequence takes a bit longer than asking the chatbot to solve it. It's also the one that matches what worked in the studies: she did the thinking, the AI asked questions, an adult checked in, and the learning got tested after a delay.

## What this looks like by age

This section is my judgment, not research. The studies above cover mostly high school students and adults, and almost nothing exists on young children. Treat it as a starting point and adjust to the learner.

- K to 2: The child doesn't use AI directly. The adult might use it to plan, to generate questions for a read-aloud, or to find activities. The learning happens with hands, books, and conversation.
- Grades 3 to 5: AI use is adult-run and done together. The child might ask a question and watch you check the answer. A good habit to build at this age is "how do we know that's true?"
- Grades 6 to 8: Guided use with clear rules. UNESCO's 2023 guidance suggested 13 as a minimum age for using generative AI in classrooms, so younger learners should work with an adult in the room. Practice the hint-only prompts and the explain-it-back check.
- Grades 9 to 12: More independence within rules you've agreed on. Use AI for feedback on drafts and for quizzing. Have the learner note what they asked and what they changed.
- College and adult learners: The risk is quiet over-reliance, since nobody is checking. Schedule AI-free retrieval practice on purpose.

Age rules also vary by tool, so read each app's terms before a child uses it.

## When the AI is wrong

Chatbots make mistakes, and they present them in the same confident voice as everything else. That's a bigger problem for learners than for experts, since a learner has less background to notice something off. Remember the Microsoft and Carnegie Mellon finding: people who were less sure of a task were more likely to take the AI's answer as adequate.

Teach checking as a skill. Ask the learner where the claim could be verified, then have them go and look. Use the planted-mistake prompt above. When the AI gives a fact, a date, a quote, or a source, treat it as a lead to confirm, and be extra careful with citations, because AI tools can invent sources that sound real.

## Cheating, detectors, and what to do instead

It's tempting to settle suspicion with an AI detector. Please don't treat the result as proof. A Stanford study published in the journal Patterns ran seven widely used detectors on 91 essays that Chinese students had written for the TOEFL exam. The detectors labeled more than half of them as AI-generated, an average false-positive rate of 61.3%. The same detectors classified 88 essays by US eighth graders accurately. The authors concluded that the tools tend to penalize writers with a narrower range of words and phrasing, and they could be fooled by simple prompting too.

A better approach is to look at process. Ask the learner to explain a paragraph out loud. Look at drafts and notes. Give a short in-person follow-up on the same material. If the learner can do the thing, it doesn't much matter which tool touched the first draft, and if they can't, you've found out without an accusation.

## For parents and teachers

Say what's allowed for each assignment, in plain words. A simple three-level scheme works:

- No AI. Used for retrieval practice, first drafts, and anything you're using to find out what the learner knows.
- AI for ideas and feedback. The learner does the work and can ask for hints, questions, or comments on their draft.
- AI allowed with a note. The learner can use it more freely but has to say how.

Learners who know the line are less likely to cross it by accident. Here's a short note they can attach to work from the third level:

```
AI use note
What I asked the AI:
What it gave me:
What I changed or rejected, and why:
What I can do now without it:
```

The last line is the important one. If the answer is "nothing," the AI did the learning.

Look at the process as well as the finished work. Ask the learner to explain an answer out loud, show earlier drafts, or solve a similar problem without help. Those checks show learning that a polished page can hide.

If you homeschool, you're the teacher and the person grading, which makes the checks easier and the temptation to skip them stronger. Build one regular check into the week, such as a Friday oral recap or a short quiz with no screens, and keep it the same each week so it stays routine.

Use AI where it saves the adult time and doesn't replace the learner's effort. Drafting a lesson sequence, finding materials, and adjusting reading level are good examples. The activities and assessments in those lessons are for the learner to do.

## Privacy and age rules

UNESCO's 2023 guidance on generative AI in education called on governments to regulate it, including by setting age limits and putting guardrails on data protection and user privacy. You don't have to wait for a government to do that at home.

Don't paste in names, school information, or photos of a child. On this site, learner profiles are saved in your browser, but if you pick a learner when you generate a curriculum, their name, education level, and description are sent to the AI model along with your request. Write those descriptions with that in mind. "Reads at a fourth-grade level, learns well with hands-on projects" says what the AI needs without saying who the child is.

## How this shapes Create-A-Curriculum

The site makes drafts for adults, and I want the drafts treated that way. The Muse curriculum form starts on "propose only," with the guardrail "AI proposes, the teacher disposes." You approve everything before it's final.

Do the same with a curriculum generated on the site. Read every lesson. Cut what doesn't fit your learner. Check the materials list against what you own or can get. Swap in your own activities where you have better ones. Curriculums export as PDF or Word, so you can mark one up and keep your edited copy.

Each generated curriculum includes activities and assessments, and those belong to the learner. Don't let the AI do them. If you want to turn an assessment into practice, have the learner take it without AI, then check it together.

Learner profiles are there so a curriculum can match someone's level and pacing. A lesson pitched at the right difficulty makes the learner work. A lesson that's too easy or too hard gets skipped or copied, and that's where the shortcuts start.

## Common questions

### Is using AI cheating?

It depends on the rules for that assignment, which is why the rules need to be stated ahead of time. Beyond the rules, the question to ask is whether the learner did the thinking the assignment was meant to build.

### Should I ban it?

Bans are easy to announce and hard to enforce, and they leave learners without practice using the tools well. I'd still make some work AI-free on purpose, particularly first drafts and retrieval practice. This is a judgment call, and I don't know of good studies comparing bans with guided use.

### My learner already uses it for everything. Where do I start?

Pick one kind of assignment, such as math practice or the first draft of a paragraph, and agree on one rule for it. Add the explain-it-back check. Once that feels normal, add another.

### Will my learner fall behind if they don't use AI?

Using AI well depends on knowing enough to judge its output. One of the PNAS study's authors has said the worry is that students who lean on the tool won't build the foundations they need to use it effectively later. Skills built without AI make the skills built with it stronger.

## Where I'm unsure

The research is young. Most studies run for weeks or a semester, so we don't know what happens over years. The Harvard and Nigeria results depend heavily on how the tools were built and supervised, and I can't promise that the free chatbot your learner already uses behaves like those tutors. Several of the concerning findings come from adults or from self-reports. Learners differ, and an approach that works for a motivated high schooler may not work for a seven-year-old. Tools also change every few months, which can make a study out of date quickly.

I also built an AI tool, so I have a stake in how this turns out. I'd rather say I'm unsure than pretend there's a formula. What I'm confident about is the direction: if a tool does the thinking, the learner doesn't get the benefit of it.

## A quick check before using AI on schoolwork

- Did the learner make a real attempt first?
- Is the AI giving hints and questions, or finished answers?
- Could the learner explain the result with the screen off?
- Has someone checked the facts against another source?
- Will the learner try something similar again in a day or two, without help?
- Is any personal information being pasted in?
- If the AI disappeared tomorrow, could the learner still do this?
